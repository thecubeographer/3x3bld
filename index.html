    Cycles, twists and parity

    Nearly every solve that ends one piece wrong died at one of these four spots.

    1. The buffer coming home does not mean you are done

    You stop only when the sticker sitting in the buffer slot is the buffer's own letter (A for corners, B for edges). If the sticker there is a different letter of the same piece (E or R for corners, M for edges), the buffer is home but twisted. Either way, look around the cube. Any piece still wrong means you break in.

    2. Breaking in costs one extra algorithm, and it is the last one

    Pick any wrong piece and shoot to one of its stickers. Call that letter X. Read the new sticker in the buffer and keep going as normal. That chain ends when the buffer piece comes back home, and the shot that brings it home is aimed at the slot you broke into. It will be X or one of X's sibling letters. Read it off the cube, do not assume it is X. The sibling tells you the twist.

    So a break-in through three wrong pieces is four letters, not three. On the Solution sheet every break-in is bracketed and its closing letter is marked, so you can count them before you start.

    3. A corner in the right place but turned is two shots on the same piece

    Break in on one of its stickers, then the very next target is another sticker of that same piece. Two letters, two algorithms, one corner. The Solution sheet labels these twist. The same rule flips an edge that is in place but backwards.

    4. Parity is decided by one count

    Count your corner letters. Odd means parity: run the parity algorithm once after the last corner and before the first edge. Even means skip it. That is the whole rule. The edge count will always match, so you never need to count both.

    On the 2x2

    There are no edges, so there is never parity and an odd count is fine. The down back left corner (letters H, S, X) is your fixed reference and is never a target. If a solve ends with that corner wrong, you turned the cube during the solve. Everything else about cycles and twists is identical to the 3x3.

    Check yourself before you touch it

    Letters equal algorithms. Open the Solution sheet after you memorise, type your letters into the memo check, and compare the count. If yours is short by one, the missing letter is almost always the close of a break-in.
